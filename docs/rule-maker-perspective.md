### Audience

This section is intended for a diverse audience that includes rule makers from smaller jurisdictions, campus administrators, toll road operators, and other entities that manage localized transportation regulations but do not necessarily control their own METR systems. It is also valuable for IT professionals, transportation planners, and consultants who support these entities in transitioning to METR. Additionally, regional authorities may find this section useful as they collaborate with smaller entities to ensure consistent and interoperable implementation of METR standards.

The purpose of this guide is to provide these entities with the necessary framework and strategies for participating in a METR system, ensuring that their local regulations are accurately represented and effectively integrated. By following the guidance provided here, rule makers can efficiently transition their regulation sets into an electronic format, enabling seamless interoperability with metropolitan-area, national, and multi-national METR systems.

## Regulation Conversion Priority

This section provides guidance on how rule makers should prioritize the conversion of their existing regulations into an electronic format compatible with METR. The prioritization process should consider the current state of the regulations, for example whether they are currently in a format intended for humans or machines. Additionally, the severity and legislative impact of the regulations should be factored into the decision-making process. Rule makers are encouraged to leverage existing materials wherever possible to expedite the conversion process—such as using GIS data that already represents infrastructure elements like stop bars. The goal is to make strategic investments that maximize the benefits of converting regulations while minimizing costs and disruptions.

## Inter-related Regulations

When converting regulations to an electronic format, it's important to consider how different regulation categories may be interrelated. For example, certain terms or definitions must be established before related regulations can be accurately translated. For instance, defining what constitutes a "bus" in a jurisdiction is essential before designating specific lanes as bus-only. This section outlines the importance of sequencing the conversion of regulations to avoid inconsistencies and ensure that all necessary foundational terms and relationships are in place before more complex regulations are implemented.

## Data Quality

Conforming to the METR regulation system's data quality standards is critical for ensuring the reliability and effectiveness of the regulations once they are converted into an electronic format. This section explains the importance of establishing and maintaining high data quality and how the certification level of the data should be recorded alongside the data itself. By adhering to these standards, rule makers can ensure that their regulations are trusted and correctly interpreted within the METR system.

## Trustworthy Regulation Sets

Before claiming that a regulation category is fully represented and trustworthy within a defined area, rule makers must ensure that all relevant regulations within that category are provided to ensure that both necessary and sufficient information is provided so that METR can continuously sustain trustworthiness amidst changing conditions. Jurisdictions can define sub-areas, called "defined areas", to allow a phased implementation where different sub-areas add support for different regulation categories to meet specific needs. This approach allows for quicker realization of METR benefits while still ensuring that each area is able to publicize the regulations it has available while informing users of which regulations have been implemented. The regional implementation policy should guide which regulation categories need to be addressed first and establish minimum requirements for support.

## Evolution of Regulation Sets

As new regulations are created or existing regulations change, it’s essential to have processes in place to manage this evolution. This section discusses how METR systems can accommodate new regulations&mdash;such as the installation of new stop signs&mdash;temporary regulations like planned parking restrictions, and emergent regulations such as rolling lane closures. By planning for these scenarios, rule makers can ensure that their systems remain up-to-date and responsive to changing conditions, maintaining the integrity and effectiveness of the METR system.

## Planning for Evolution of the METR Standards

METR standards are likely to evolve over time, particularly as new vehicle types emerge and as transportation technologies advance. This section emphasizes the importance of anticipating these changes when defining current regulations. Rule makers should consider how future developments might impact their regulation sets and prepare to adapt their electronic regulations accordingly, ensuring long-term compatibility and effectiveness within the METR framework.

## Operational Impacts to My Organization

The need for an electronic representation of regulations within METR encourages organizations to adopt electronic processes for regulation creation from the outset. This section explores the operational benefits of this approach, such as using electronic records to issue work orders for new signs, which aligns with the METR requirement for electronic documentation. Agencies should evaluate how this shift towards electronic regulation-making may affect various aspects of their operations and consider adopting more structured and rigorous processes to ensure their regulations can be effectively represented in METR-standard formats.

## Importance of Connectivity

For emergent regulations to be effective, they must be distributed to impacted users no later than their implementation. This requires a robust and reliable connectivity infrastructure that spans the entire communication chain. This section discusses the importance of ensuring high levels of connectivity, which may involve deploying roadside beacons or redundant communication systems to guarantee that all users receive emergent regulations in a timely fashion. Reliable connectivity is crucial for maintaining the safety, efficiency, and trustworthiness of transportation systems under METR.

## Migration from Existing Data Sources

Rule makers have access to various existing data sources that can be leveraged in the transition to METR. These sources include paper records, PDF documents, physical signage, GIS data, and electronic records maintained by the agency or private entities such as navigation systems. By utilizing these resources, agencies can improve the accuracy of their data and reduce the costs associated with implementing METR. This section outlines strategies for effectively migrating existing data into the METR system, ensuring a smooth and cost-efficient transition.

## Minimum Requirements for Interoperability

To achieve interoperability within METR, rule makers must adhere to specific standards and verify their proper implementation. This section identifies the key standards that need to be considered and explains the importance of working with regional authorities to ensure alignment with regional policies. By meeting these minimum requirements, rule makers can ensure that their regulations are compatible with the broader METR system, facilitating seamless integration and cooperation across different jurisdictions.

## Data Quality Assurance

Ensuring data quality is paramount for the success of the METR system. This section provides an overview of the options available for maintaining data quality in line with METR-defined certification levels. It highlights the importance of continuous data quality assurance and outlines the processes that rule makers can implement to maintain high standards, ensuring that their electronic regulations are accurate, reliable, and trusted by all stakeholders.

## Tailoring the Guide and the Standards

While this guide provides a comprehensive framework for METR implementation, it is essential for deployment sites to tailor the guidance and standards to their specific needs. This section highlights the key aspects that may require customization, including the prioritization of regulation categories, data quality requirements, and operational impacts. By adapting the guide to local conditions, rule makers can ensure that their METR implementation is both effective and sustainable, meeting the unique needs of their jurisdictions.
